Global Citizenship and Community Impact: Notes from Abu Dhabi

📅 November 19, 2026
✎ Darrell Seale
⌚ 3 min read

Darrell Seale has visited 142 countries, but the place that has shaped his idea of citizenship most is the one where he chose to stay. The retired international business executive moved to Abu Dhabi in 2014 and has made the city’s community his own.

In Seale’s case, global citizenship has looked less like an abstraction than a set of habits: learning how a place works, contributing to it, and respecting the people who were there first. Those habits were formed over a career that began in the United States Air Force and continued through two decades in aerospace and defense, and they became more deliberate after retirement.

Seeing the World Firsthand

Travel at Seale’s scale is a long project. Reaching 142 countries has taken him from the summit of Mount Kilimanjaro, at 5,895 meters the highest point in Africa, to penguin colonies on the Antarctic coast. Many of those journeys are shared on Darrell Seale’s YouTube channel.

What that breadth of travel builds is not expertise in any single culture but a habit of humility. Customs that seem obvious in one country are unusual in the next, and a traveler learns quickly that good intentions do not substitute for understanding. That lesson transferred directly to international business, where partnerships depend on recognizing how decisions are actually made in a given place.

Global citizenship is measured less by the number of passport stamps than by what a person contributes where they live.

Choosing to Stay and Contribute

When Seale retired after more than twenty years in the defense sector, he remained in Abu Dhabi. He takes part in his building’s homeowners’ association, joins local sports, cultural, and recreational activities, and continues teaching scuba diving in the UAE. Earlier, from 2018 to 2019, he served on the board of directors of the American Chamber of Commerce Abu Dhabi.

This kind of involvement is unglamorous by design. Homeowners’ meetings, sports leagues, and dive lessons rarely make news, but they are where residents get to know one another and where trust in a shared community is built. For a resident who arrived through a corporate posting, staying engaged after the job ended was a deliberate choice.

Inclusion in Practice: The 2019 Special Olympics

One of the clearest expressions of that choice came in 2019, when Abu Dhabi hosted the Special Olympics World Games, the first held in the Middle East and North Africa. Darrell Ray Seale competed on a Unified Sports volleyball team, a format that places athletes with and without intellectual disabilities on the same roster, and he presented medals to more than 50 athletes during the Games.

Unified Sports reflects a principle that also runs through his work with veterans: inclusion is something people do together, not something offered from a distance. The same idea underpins his years of scuba instruction for wounded veterans, where students and teammates meet on equal terms in the water.

Conclusion

Global citizenship, as Seale has practiced it, starts with curiosity and ends in local commitment. The countries he has visited supplied perspective; Abu Dhabi supplied the place to use it. His wider record as a veteran, executive, and volunteer is set out in the full profile of Darrell Seale.
